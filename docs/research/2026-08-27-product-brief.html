01

Executive summary

What this is, and the five claims it rests on

This document proposes DataGrid — not a table component, but the surface almost every enterprise healthcare workflow is actually made of. A patient directory, a results inbox, a behavioural-health caseload, a bed board, a claims worklist and a medication administration record are all the same engine wearing different recipes, and today every product in this market rebuilds each of them from a generic grid plus six months of cell renderers.

The research below covers 39 healthcare scenarios across 7 families, a 75-item feature inventory split across five layers, seven competitor systems measured rather than described, 28 edge cases, a complete keyboard model, and 20 live prototypes you can operate in this page (three more are static).

The five claims this brief rests on

  1. A clinical grid is not a view of rows; it is a claim about a population. Every worklist answers “who needs something from me, and what?” — and the most dangerous property of a grid is that it looks complete whether or not it is. ADR 0011 already requires a component that shows some of a set to declare that set’s boundaries. The grid is the case that ADR was written about, and a filtered grid is a worse liar than a paginated one, because the filter was set by a human who has since forgotten it.
  2. The row is a person, so row identity is a safety control. Everywhere else rowKey is a reconciliation hint. Here, acting on the wrong row is the wrong-patient error that the NQF-endorsed retract-and-reorder measure exists to count — and identity re-entry interventions cut those errors by 30–41%. That makes selection, bulk action and inline edit identity-carrying operations, not list operations.
  3. Sorting is a clinical act. Sorting a worklist by a risk score reorders who gets seen first: that is triage. When the score is a model output, the sort silently converts the model’s quality into a queue discipline. The Epic Sepsis Model shipped to hundreds of hospitals and, on external validation, achieved an AUC of 0.63 against the 0.76–0.83 cited internally — missing two-thirds of sepsis cases while generating roughly 109 alerts per true positive. A sort by a derived column names its source in the header. Nobody else does this.
  4. Live data must never move under the hand. Standard grids re-sort on change. In a census that is a wrong-patient generator: you aim at row four and an admission inserts at row two. Sort position freezes while a pointer or focus is inside the body; arrivals queue behind a divider until you ask for them.
  5. The grid does not own the cells. Six clinical primitives already ship in this repository, and two of them — ClinicalStatus and ResultValue — already carry stories called GridAffix and InAGrid. They were built for a host that did not exist yet. The grid’s job is to be that host: a cell contract covering measurement, truncation, focus, print and export, so the catalogue drops in unchanged and stays independently useful.

02

Product vision

Why this cannot be a themed table

The brief for this component was “not a generic table with sorting and filtering”. Agreed — but that is a statement about ambition, not about architecture. The architectural claim has to be sharper, and it is this: almost every enterprise healthcare screen is a grid, and almost none of them are tables.

A table is a set of rows you read. The surfaces this component has to power are not that. A results inbox is a queue of obligations with statutory clocks. A bed board is a capacity artefact whose empty cells carry the meaning. A CoCM registry is a billable instrument. An MAR is a time axis you act on. A claims worklist is a ledger where the totals must foot. Rendering each of these as “rows with clinical words in them” is what every existing product does, and it is why every existing product needs six months of cell renderers per screen.

Three things a generic grid gets wrong, and healthcare cannot afford

It treats absence as nothing. A blank cell in AG Grid means the accessor returned undefined. In a chart it could mean not ordered, not resulted, not measured, refused, not applicable, withheld under a consent rule, or the source that would have answered timed out four seconds ago. Those are seven different next actions. This library’s founding lint rule already makes {value ?? "—"} an error; a grid that renders a thousand of those per screen is the largest possible violation of it.

It treats completeness as the caller’s problem. Roughly 13.6% of primary-care consultations proceed with clinical information missing, and it adversely affects care in about half of them; one emergency study found records missing or incomplete on admission for 27% of patients, with unnecessary procedures — lumbar punctures among them — performed on 5% as a result. The literature’s term is informative missingness: a NULL read as a negative. A grid is the highest-volume producer of that reading in any health product, and it produces it while looking perfect.

It treats configuration as chrome. Column sets, sort orders and filters are stored inside the grid as imperative API state, so “save this view” is bespoke serialisation every time, role presets are a different mechanism from saved views, and the product’s own default is a third. They are one artefact. Making them one artefact is most of what makes fifty different healthcare grids buildable from one system.

Why not just theme Ant Design’s Table

Because it was measured, and the measurement is decisive. @rc-component/table@1.11.1, the engine under antd v6’s Table, emits exactly one aria-* attribute in its entire ES build — aria-hidden — no role, and zero keyboard handlers. antd’s own layer adds aria-sort, aria-description, aria-expanded and five aria-labels. Across both packages there is no aria-rowcount, no aria-rowindex, no aria-colindex, no cell focus and no arrow-key navigation. A virtualised antd table tells a screen-reader user nothing at all about where they are in forty thousand rows.

This is the same argument that settled the Switch. ADR 0010’s rule is that primitives match Ant Design’s public API exactly and take no dependency on it, and its second reason was “nothing expensive to inherit”. rc-switch was a button with aria-checked and two key handlers. @rc-component/table is a renderer with one ARIA attribute and no key handlers. Wrapping it would mean shadowing the render to add grid semantics — paying for a dependency you route around — and it would put antd into every future data surface, ending copy-source distribution for the whole family.

Keep the API. Replace the semantics. Same columns, same dataSource, same onChange(pagination, filters, sorter), with a props-parity table in the test suite so an antd rename fails our build rather than a customer’s. Everything it adds is additive, and every divergence is listed in limitations.

The thing it should become

Not a component. A healthcare data interaction framework: one engine, one presentation layer, one disclosure policy, and a growing set of recipes. A recipe is data — a typed, serialisable GridView — which means the product’s default grid, the nurse’s role preset and Dr Smith’s saved view are the same artefact from three sources, resolved by a stated precedence. That single decision is what turns “build me a caseload screen” from a sprint into an afternoon.

Feature inventory

Seventy-five capabilities across five layers

The rule for the split is simple. If it can be computed without a DOM, it is engine. If it is markup, tokens, motion or ARIA, it is presentation. If most grids will never use it, it is a plugin and it costs nothing when it is not imported. If it encodes a clinical or regulatory meaning, it is the healthcare layer. 06

UX architecture

Eight interaction rules, and the row that is not a patient

Interaction cost is the whole game. Physicians spend an average of 16 minutes 14 seconds per patient encounter in the EHR, roughly a third of it on chart review, and 5.9 hours of EHR work for every 8 hours of scheduled care; primary-care physicians average 2.7 hours a day of uncompensated after-hours charting. A grid that saves four seconds per row, forty times an hour, is not a nicety.

But speed is not the only constraint, and this is the trap the category has fallen into. The FDA’s revised clinical-decision-support guidance (6 January 2026) added, as criterion 4, that software should prioritise decision-relevant details and avoid information overload — a user-interface requirement sitting inside a device-classification document. Density without hierarchy is now a regulatory concern, not only a design one.

Eight interaction rules

  1. The row is whatever the work is about. A work queue’s row is an obligation, not a patient. Two obligations on one person are two rows and must not be merged. The identity cell then becomes context rather than subject — which is why it is a cell, not a special case.
  2. Every row states what it is waiting for and from whom. The resolution cell is three facts in one column: what is owed, who owes it, when it is due. It replaces the four-column reconstruction a clinician currently performs in their head, twelve times a minute.
  3. Two identifiers before any action. The Joint Commission’s NPSG.01.01.01 requires two person-specific identifiers before a care action, and the identity package already makes fewer than two a compile error. In a grid the same rule means the identity cell carries name plus one identifier at every density — at ultra-dense the secondary line drops and the MRN moves inline, rather than the identifier being the thing that disappears.
  4. Disambiguate rather than deduplicate. Two patients sharing a name and a date of birth is not an edge case on a worklist; it is Tuesday. disambiguate() already adds the minimum that separates them and handles the newborn-twins case that colour and initials both fail.
  5. Colour is never the only cue. Every status is a glyph, a word and a rail. The contrast gate and the forced-colors tests already run across three engines, and a status that survives neither is a status that disappears in high-contrast mode — which is exactly where an at-risk reader is.
  6. Progressive disclosure has three steps, not two. Cell → row expansion → inspector → chart. Each step costs more attention and each is reversible without losing position. §13 argues where each one is correct.
  7. Never more than two chips before an overflow. A patient with eleven problems is common. Eleven chips is a colour field with a table behind it. Two plus +9, with an accessible popover and the full list in the accessible name, keeps the row scannable and keeps the count honest.
  8. A destructive or clinical action on many people shows the people. Not the count. The count is the confirmation; the names are the check. §14.

What the resolution row replaces. A conventional results inbox gives you patient, test, value, flag, ordered-by, resulted-at, status — seven columns from which the reader derives “I have seven minutes to acknowledge a potassium of 6.8 on Aisha Bello”. The derivation is the work, it happens once per row, and it is where attention leaks. Making it a column is the single highest-leverage change in this document.

Filtering architecture

One tree, six renderings, and a predicate that prints

Filtering deserves to be treated as its own component, and this section argues something stronger: there must be exactly one filter representation in the system. A FilterNode tree — a discriminated union of conditions and groups with and / or / not. The visual builder produces it. The chip bar renders it. The quick-filter pills are named instances of it. The URL serialises it. The saved view stores it. The export header prints it. The natural-language bar compiles to it and cannot express anything it cannot express.

Every product that has two filter mechanisms eventually ships two filters that disagree, and in a clinical product the disagreement is a cohort that is quietly wrong.

The second rule: a filtered grid must render its predicate in words

This is where a grid is more dangerous than the timeline ADR 0011 was written about. A paginated list is obviously partial. A filtered list looks complete, the filter was set by a human who has since forgotten it, and views restore filters set six days ago. “No results” after a filter is indistinguishable from “no patients have this problem” — and one of those is a clinical finding.

So: the active query renders as a sentence, in the coverage bar, always, and it prints. Not chips alone — chips lose the grouping, and (A or B) and C is a different cohort from A or (B and C).

Search architecture

Three surfaces, and why the grid owns only one

Three surfaces, and the recommendation is that the grid owns only one of them.

The in-grid search box is narrow and honest: it searches the columns that are visible and the identifiers that are indexed, and it says which. That last part matters — a search box that silently does not search free text is a search box that teaches a clinician the note does not exist.

Column search lives in the header menu and produces a filter condition. There is nothing special about it beyond scoping.

The command palette is not the grid’s. ChartCommandPalette already ships in this repository, at Stable, with scoped search, frequency weighting, an out-of-scope count, and a break-glass-aware unavailable state. Building a second palette inside the grid would produce two search surfaces with different behaviour, and the one people learn would be whichever they hit first. The grid should compose it: press Ctrl+K anywhere in a grid and the palette opens scoped to that grid, with the grid’s own actions registered as commands.

Real-time updates

The ground never moves under a human who is aiming at it

The rule is one sentence: the ground never moves under a human who is aiming at it. Everything else in this section follows from it.

The failure it prevents is concrete and it is a patient-safety event, not an annoyance. A charge nurse moves the pointer toward row four of a census. An admission arrives and sorts to row two. The click lands on the wrong patient. That is the same class of error the wrong-patient retract-and-reorder measure counts, and it has a UI cause rather than a human one.

So: while a pointer is over the grid body, or keyboard focus is inside it, sort position is frozen. Updates to visible cells still apply, because a stale value is its own hazard; what is suspended is reordering and insertion. Arrivals queue behind a divider with a count, and inserting them is the reader’s action, on the reader’s timing.

The other half: never look live when you are not

A grid that keeps rendering relative timestamps after its socket has dropped is lying with confidence. Three behaviours, all cheap:

  • A connection state in the header — live, reconnecting, or last updated 09:41. Never nothing.
  • Relative time becomes absolute past the staleness budget. A row that says 2 minutes ago for forty minutes is worse than one that says 09:41.
  • A reconnect refreshes and says so, because the gap is exactly where a missed event lives, and the coverage sentence is the only honest place to put that.

Conflict resolution takes the Switch’s rule, unchanged. Two clinicians edit the same row: the second write is refused, both values are shown, and there is no preselection. Last-write-wins discards the first clinician’s reason; first-write-wins discards a deliberate more recent change. Neither is safe to choose automatically, and the Switch has already argued this at length.

The rule an expansion must not break

This is ADR 0011 again, one level down: a component that shows some of a set declares the set.

The inspector should be a platform pattern, not a grid feature

“A list on the left, a detail panel on the right, one URL, no navigation” is not specific to grids. It is the right shell for a timeline, a document list, a claim, a message thread and a work queue. The recommendation is that it ships as InspectorLayout in the composition layer with the grid as its first consumer — otherwise the second consumer copies it, and the two drift on focus management, which is the part that is hard.

  • Selecting a row does not move focus. Focus stays in the grid so ↓ moves to the next row and the panel follows. Working down forty rows is one keystroke each, not four.
  • The panel is reachable in one keystroke and returns in one. F6 cycles landmarks; Esc in the panel returns focus to the row it describes.
  • It is not a dialog. No focus trap, no modal semantics, no inert background — the grid stays operable, because operating the grid is the point.

Bulk clinical workflows

A selection is a predicate, not a list of ticked boxes

Bulk actions are where a grid stops being a view and becomes a write surface, and it is where the failure modes get expensive. Three rules.

1. A selection is a predicate, not a list of ticked boxes

The value is { predicate, includedIds, excludedIds }. That shape is what makes “select all 240,000 matching” expressible at all, and — more importantly — it is what lets the confirmation step re-resolve the selection and notice that a row left the predicate while the user was deciding. Without it, that check is impossible and a bulk write silently applies to rows that no longer qualify.

Select-all is two affordances, not one. Ctrl+A and the header checkbox select the rows on this page and say so. Selecting every matching row is a separate, explicitly worded action — “Select all 240,000 rows matching this query” — because the two have completely different consequences and one of them is a page-sized accident.

2. A clinical or destructive action shows the people, not the count

The count is the confirmation; the names are the check. A reviewer who reads “12 patients” confirms a number. A reviewer who reads twelve names notices the one that should not be there. This costs one card and it is the single cheapest safety control in the document.

3. Not every action should be bulk-able

Some are refused on purpose, and the refusal is the design.

A grid that reports “error” has thrown away the nine, and one that reports “success” has hidden the three. The result is a per-row outcome set rendered back into the grid: three rows marked with their reason, an action to retry only those, and the nine left alone. Anything less makes the user re-derive what happened by reading the data.

Saved views, roles and personalisation

One artefact from six sources

This is the section with the highest leverage per line of code, and it rests on one decision: the product’s default grid, the role preset and the user’s saved view are the same artefact from three sources.

One serialisable document — GridView — carrying columns, widths, order, pinning, visibility, sort, filter tree, grouping, density and page size. Not the data. Never the data.

Every commercial grid gets this wrong in the same way: view state lives inside the grid as imperative API state, so saving a view is bespoke serialisation, role presets are a second mechanism, and the product default is a third. Making it one artefact means role-based grids, saved views, URL sharing, defaults and admin-locked column sets are one feature, and it is the reason fifty healthcare grids become tractable from one system.

Responsive strategy

Column priority, declared once, read four times

“Do not simply shrink the desktop grid” is right, but the useful version of that instruction is a mechanism rather than a warning: column priority is a property of the column, declared once, and four different consumers read it. Responsive drop order, print column set, export column order, and what survives into a card — all one number.

Three transformations, and why the middle one is the hard one

  • Desktop → tablet is column dropping by priority plus the inspector. Mechanical.
  • Tablet → phone is the hard one, because it is a change of form, not of size. A row becomes a card, columns become a title, a chip and two fact lines, and the ordering is priority order. The trap is that a card invites more information than a row did; the discipline is that a card shows fewer facts than the tablet grid, not more.
  • Anything → print drops every interactive affordance and gains two: the coverage sentence and the predicate, on every page.

The reflow requirement is not negotiable and it is already tested here. WCAG 2.2 requires content to reflow at 320px equivalent width, and this repository already runs 320px reflow and 200% zoom across three engines. A grid is the component most likely to break it, so the rule is explicit: the page never scrolls horizontally; only the grid’s own scroller does. A grid that makes the whole document scroll sideways has failed, however well it renders.

Density and visual variants

Four densities, seven variants, one token surface

Four densities, and the rule that makes them a system rather than a preference: a density that breaks a floor is not offered. Not discouraged — removed from the control. Type never goes below 12px, the contrast floors hold at every step, and pointer targets stay at 24px minimum (44px on touch), so ultra-dense cannot be selected on a touch device where its targets would fail.

Accessibility architecture

AI opportunities

Four rules, seven capabilities, ranked by harm

Four rules, and they are the whole section. Everything else is application.

  1. AI output is a provenance state of a value, not a kind of column. The catalogue already has the states: ClinicalStatus has AI draft, ResultValue has AI extracted, and ProvenanceChip has extracted, extracted and confirmed, and the whole chain. Nothing new is needed to render AI honestly. Anything that does invent a new treatment for it is creating a second vocabulary for the same idea.
  2. Never mix generated and verified values in one cell. A cell is one provenance. A row may mix; a cell may not, because a reader reads a cell as one fact.
  3. Distinguishable without colour. A glyph and a word, like every other status in this library. An AI treatment that is a purple tint disappears in forced-colors mode, which is exactly where it must not.
  4. The proposal channel never writes. AI may propose a filter, a column, a grouping or an action. Applying it is always a human act, and the applied thing is always the same kind of object a human could have made by hand — which is what makes it reviewable and reversible.

The strongest argument for all of this is a published one. The Epic Sepsis Model was deployed at hundreds of hospitals; external validation found an AUC of 0.63 against the 0.76–0.83 cited internally, missing two-thirds of sepsis cases and generating enough false alarms to require roughly 109 alerts per true positive. And alert-override rates in clinical decision support run 46–96% across studies, with acceptance dropping about 30% for each additional reminder in an encounter. A grid that adds a model column without adding the model’s provenance is contributing to exactly that, at scale, in the surface clinicians look at most.

Which is why the sort-provenance banner is not a nicety. It is the one place in a worklist where a model’s quality can be read at the moment it is being used to decide who is seen first.

Why not build on TanStack Table v9

Fourteen slices, four owners, and one rule that decides where each lives: if it can be serialised into a saved view, it belongs to the engine. Everything else is session or derived.

The engine is a reducer over a discriminated union of actions. Not because reducers are fashionable, but because three properties fall out of it that a grid genuinely needs: every state transition is a value that can be logged, replayed and property-tested; the whole engine is testable with no renderer; and undo/redo is a stack of actions rather than a bespoke feature.

Two rules that prevent the classic grid bugs

  • Data is never state. The grid does not own rows; it renders what it is given and asks for more through one contract. This is what makes client mode and server mode the same consumer API, and it is what stops a “refresh” button from being subtly different from a filter change.
  • Derived values are selectors, not stored fields. The visible row window, the group tree, the summary row and the chip set are all derived through memoised, layered selectors. A stored derived field is a cache invalidation bug with a date on it.

Where the query lives is deliberately not our decision. The grid takes a GridQuery and returns a GridPage; whether that is TanStack Query, SWR, RTK Query, a plain fetch or a SMART-on-FHIR client is the application’s business. The grid supplies the cache key — the serialised GridQuery — which is the only part it is uniquely able to get right, and it is the same value the URL and the saved view use.

Ten examples, in the order a developer meets them

The test for this API is the one this repository already applies: a simple grid should be four lines, and every advanced capability should be reachable without leaving the type system. Ten examples, in the order a developer meets them.

7 · Filters are one tree

type FilterNode =
  | { op: "and" | "or"; children: FilterNode[] }
  | { op: "not"; child: FilterNode }

// Every producer returns this shape and nothing else:
filterFromChips(chips)          // the chip bar
filterFromBuilder(builderState) // the visual builder
filterFromUrl(searchParams)     // a shared link

The natural-language compiler returns a result type, not a node. It cannot fail silently, and it cannot approximate — an unmatched clause is returned so the UI can say what it could not understand.

<DataGrid

interface DisclosurePolicy {
  column(col: string): "visible" | "withheld";
  cell(row: unknown, col: string): "visible" | { masked: { reason: string; legal?: string } };
  row(row: unknown): "visible" | { restricted: { reason: string; breakGlass: boolean } };

  mayPrint(): boolean;
  mayCopy(): boolean;
}

Loading, empty and error states

Six empty states, because there are six facts

These are not afterthoughts here; three of them are the reason the component exists. A grid spends a meaningful share of its life not showing rows, and every one of those moments is a chance to say something false.

Four more, and the rule for each

  • Refreshing. The old rows stay, dimmed, with a progress indicator — never a skeleton. Replacing content the reader is using with a skeleton is a regression disguised as feedback, and it loses their place.
  • Offline. The grid keeps rendering what it has and stamps it: “offline since 09:41 — this is what you had then”. It does not clear, and it does not pretend.
  • Timeout. Distinct from an error, because the action differs: a timeout offers a retry with a longer budget and a narrower query. An error offers a report.
  • Restricted. Its own state, not an empty state. “3 people match, and you may not see them” — the count is disclosed because hiding it lets a filter be used to probe who exists.

The one rule that unifies all of them. Every state answers what do I now know that I did not before? “No data” answers nothing. “No rows match this query, and here is the query” answers it. That is why there are six empty states rather than one, and it is the same argument the timeline made for its three.

Testing strategy

Assert the behaviour, not the hook

The convention this repository already holds is that a test asserts the safety claim the component exists to make, not that it renders — because the failures worth catching all look fine on screen. For a grid that principle is load-bearing, because a grid can be wrong in a way that is invisible to every automated check: it can render forty perfect rows and be showing the wrong forty.

And there is a specific lesson on file worth repeating here. The Timeline review found six real defects with every CI gate green, and three shared one shape: a test that asserts a class name passes while the class does nothing. A grid has more of those hooks than anything else in the catalogue. Assert the behaviour, not the hook — and look at the rendered page.

The eight most dangerous tests to be missing

  1. A masked cell exports masked. One assertion; without it, the mask is a render concern and the CSV is a breach.
  2. A live update does not move the row under the pointer. Property test, and it is the wrong-patient one.
  3. A stale selection is caught before a bulk write. Rows leave the predicate between selection and confirmation.
  4. Absolute aria-rowindex at row 19,998 of 40,000. The defect every virtualised grid has.
  5. Every operation reachable by keyboard, asserted with no pointer events dispatched.
  6. Aggregation refuses mixed units, asserted in words rather than by exception type.
  7. A permission change while open removes columns and announces it.
  8. A source that timed out produces an alert, not a quiet short list.

It is what lets a customer assert that their grid announces what it should, which is the only way this scales past the components we write ourselves.

Competitor comparison

Seven systems, measured where measurement was possible

Seven systems. Where a claim could be measured rather than described, it was — the Ant Design rows come from reading antd@6.6.0 and @rc-component/table@1.11.1 in node_modules, not from the documentation, which disagrees with the source often enough to be unreliable.

One finding is worth stating before the table. Terra UI, Cerner’s React design system and the only serious healthcare component library, was archived on 28 June 2024 along with the whole Cerner GitHub organisation. There is no maintained healthcare grid to benchmark against. The category is abandoned with residual demand — which reframes the strategy from “compete” to “occupy”.

AG Grid36.1.0 · 5 Aug 2026Community MIT · Enterprise US$999/dev perpetual (1 yr updates); bundle with AG Charts US$1,598
What it does well
  • The deepest feature set in the category, and the only one whose virtualisation is genuinely battle-tested at millions of rows: rows and columns both windowed, a configurable rowBuffer, and node recycling.
  • Server-side row model with grouping, aggregation and pivot pushed to the server — the only one that treats the server as the source of truth by default.
  • Framework-agnostic core with thin adapters, so behaviour does not fork per framework.
What it does poorly
  • Enterprise licensing is per developer, which is a procurement conversation before the first prototype.
  • The API is imperative and enormous (a grid API object with hundreds of methods); state lives inside the grid, so 'save this view' is a bespoke serialise/restore each time.
  • Styling is a theming system rather than your design system; matching an existing token surface is an ongoing tax.
  • No domain semantics at all — every clinical meaning is a cellRenderer you write and maintain.

TanStack Tablev9 stable · 4 Aug 2026MIT
What it does well
  • Genuinely headless: the state and row-model pipeline with no opinion about markup, which is the correct shape for a design system to build on.
  • v9 is modular and tree-shaken — register only the features and row models you use; ~25 KB min+brotli for the full package, with up to 90% memory savings on large tables.
  • Adapters now bind to each framework's native reactivity rather than React-shaped assumptions.
What it does poorly
  • Headless means you own every accessibility decision, and most consumers get that wrong — there is no keyboard model in the box.
  • No virtualisation (it is a separate library), so the two hardest problems — row models and windowing — are solved in different repositories with different mental models.
  • Server-side operations are a convention, not a contract.

  • The cleanest declarative API of the commercial grids, and a genuinely good default look.

  • Premium now bundles an AI assistant and charts integration, which is where the category is heading.
What it does poorly
  • The free/paid boundary runs straight through table stakes: column pinning, tree data and row grouping are all paid, so the free grid is a demo.
  • Tied to MUI's styling engine and theme, which is a second design system inside yours.
  • Row-level semantics stop at `getRowClassName` — the same clinical-meaning-as-CSS-class problem.

  • Excellent API ergonomics — `columns` + `dataSource` + `onChange` is still the most teachable table API in React, which is exactly why we match it.
  • `virtual` with `scroll.x/y` works, backed by @rc-component/virtual-list, which does measure dynamic row heights via ResizeObserver and a height cache.

  • No cell-level focus and no arrow-key navigation: a keyboard user reaches a table's contents only through whatever interactive elements happen to be inside cells.

  • Column sets per role are the norm and clinicians expect them, which validates recipes over bespoke screens.
  • Status columns carry glyph + colour + text, because these products learned the hard way that colour alone fails.
  • Patient identity in a worklist row routinely carries two identifiers, matching NPSG.01.01.01.
What it does poorly
  • Information density has drifted into information overload; the FDA's revised CDS guidance (6 Jan 2026) now explicitly asks software to prioritise decision-relevant details and avoid overload — a UI requirement inside a device-classification document.
  • Risk scores are shown as bare numbers. The Epic Sepsis Model's external validation (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021) found an AUC of 0.63 against the 0.76–0.83 cited internally, missing two-thirds of sepsis cases while generating enough false alarms to require ~109 alerts per true positive — a number that a column of bare scores makes impossible to reason about.
  • Terra UI, Cerner's React design system and the only serious healthcare component library, was archived on 28 June 2024 along with the Cerner GitHub organisation. There is no maintained healthcare grid to copy from.
What Oxygen can do better: Make the model's own quality visible where the sort happens, and make 'what am I not seeing' a permanent, printed part of the surface.

Implementation roadmap

Seven phases, 23–29 weeks, Phase 1 ships alone

Seven phases, 23–29 weeks. The brief proposed six; this adds one and reorders two, for reasons worth stating.

  • Coverage and the absence taxonomy move into Phase 1, not a later “healthcare layer”. They are the type system. Retrofitting a required prop and a discriminated absence union onto a shipped API is a breaking change, and retrofitting them onto a shipped mental model is worse.
  • Accessibility is not a hardening phase. The keyboard model and the ARIA contract are Phase 1, because they determine the DOM structure. A grid that adds role="grid" in month five is a grid that rewrites its focus management in month five.
  • Disclosure gets its own phase. The brief folded it into hardening. It is a policy engine feeding six behaviours and it deserves four weeks and its own acceptance criteria — and it is the phase most likely to be mistaken for a compliance guarantee, so the limitations copy is written before the code.
  • Intelligence is second-to-last, not last. Hardening must come after it, because an AI surface that has not been through the accessibility and visual-regression gates is exactly the surface that ships a purple tint as its only signal.

Eight open questions

Six carry a recommendation. Two are blocked on something only you can supply.

Is the grid free or the first paid pack?my recommendation
The catalogue is currently 100% `tier: "free"` and the marketplace prices packs, not components. A data grid is the single most valuable thing in this library and the most expensive to build — roughly 23–29 weeks by the plan above. My recommendation: **the engine, the presentation layer, the keyboard model and the whole safety surface are free forever; the paid pack is the recipes** (MAR, flowsheet, claims ledger, cohort) plus `grid-testing`. It keeps the SEO and trust value, and it prices the part that is genuinely bespoke labour.
Does `coverage` stay required here?my recommendation
ADR 0011 says yes and the grid is the case the ADR was actually about. But a grid is a far more common surface than a timeline, and this is the prop most likely to make someone choose a different library. My recommendation: **required, no escape hatch**, same as `CareTimeline` — but ship a `coverage.local()` helper for the honest single-source case so the minimum is one line rather than four.
Is the transposed grid the same component?my recommendation
Flowsheet and MAR invert the axes: analytes down, time across, header scrolling. That is either a `layout` prop or a second component. My recommendation: **one component, `layout="transposed"`**, decided at the end of Phase 3 on evidence — but I want the decision recorded as provisional, because getting it wrong is a public API break.
What is the row-count constant past which client mode refuses?blocked on you
I have written it as a constant rather than a judgement call, which is right, but I have not chosen the number. It should be measured on the CI machine at the density where the budget breaks, not picked. Blocking on Phase 2's benchmark.
Do we take a dependency on TanStack Table v9 for the engine?my recommendation
It is MIT, modular, ~25 KB, and genuinely good — and v9's memory work is exactly our problem. Against: ADR 0009's supply-chain constraints, copy-source distribution needs self-contained files, and our row model has to carry disclosure and absence semantics that its row model has no place for. My recommendation: **no dependency, but read its row-model pipeline closely** — the same conclusion ADR 0010 reached about rc-switch, for the same reason.
Who is the named clinician reviewer, and at what rate?blocked on you
This is the third brief to ask. It now blocks the behavioural-health recipes, the MAR, and the safety copy in Phase 5 — all of which state clinical rules I derived from literature rather than from a clinician's screen.
Does the grid own search, or compose `ChartCommandPalette`?my recommendation
The palette already ships and already solves scoped search, frequency weighting and the out-of-scope count. My recommendation: **compose it**, and let the grid's own search box be the narrow, in-place one. Two search surfaces with different behaviour would be worse than either.
Is `DataGrid` the right name given ADR 0010?my recommendation
antd's component is `Table`. Ours matches its API but is not it — it is `role="grid"`, cell-focusable and virtualised by default. Shipping a `Table` that behaves differently from antd's `Table` would break the parity promise. My recommendation: **`DataGrid`**, with the props-parity table asserted against antd's `Table` and the divergences named in `limitations` — and a one-line codemod in `@oxygenui-design/codemod` for people migrating from antd.

Final recommendations

  1. Build it, and build the engine first. The grid is the single most valuable component this library can ship and the one with the clearest differentiation, because the category has no healthcare-native incumbent — Terra UI is archived and nothing replaced it.
  2. Ship Phase 1 as its own release. Four weeks to something a product can build a patient directory on, that already tells the truth. Then stop, use it, and let the second phase be informed by that.
  3. Keep the whole safety surface free, forever, in every tier. Coverage, absence, the keyboard model, the ARIA contract, masking. Gate the recipes and grid-testing if anything is gated. A safety control behind a paywall is a bad look and a worse product.
  4. Do not chase AG Grid on features. Chase it on honesty. The bottom seven rows of the comparison matrix are the product.
  5. Get a clinician reviewer before Phase 3. It now blocks three briefs. Until then, everything clinical in this document is a well-researched proposal and should be labelled as one.
  6. Write the limitations copy for Phase 5 before writing Phase 5. It is the cheapest possible insurance against the one risk here that is marked critical and is not about patients.
